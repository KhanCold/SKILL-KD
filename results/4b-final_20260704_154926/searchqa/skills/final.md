[RULE 001] Prefer categorical subtypes for type questions
content: When a question asks for a 'type' or 'kind' of something and multiple candidates appear in context, prefer a distinct categorical subtype (a named variety) over a temporally or qualitatively modified version of the parent category.
why: Both student and teacher answered 'modern chewing gum' when the gold was 'bubble gum' — 'modern' is a temporal modifier of the parent category, not a distinct type, while 'bubble gum' is a categorical subtype that properly fits the 'this type of' question framing.

[RULE 002] Parse 'this' question targets correctly
content: When a question contains 'this' (either as 'this [noun]' or as a standalone pronoun) referring to an entity in a described relationship, identify what grammatical role that pronoun plays (e.g., the thing being parodied, imitated, replaced, or the source of energy) and answer with the entity filling that role, not a related entity like the parody name, work title, modifier, or measurement type. When 'this' appears as a standalone pronoun at the end of a question (the question target), the answer is the entity 'this' refers to — do not apply redundancy-stripping to the answer entity itself. When 'this [noun]' refers to a site, place, or container and other entities are described as being within or part of it (e.g., 'a building at this site'), the 'this [noun]' entity is the answer — do not answer with the contained entity, which is merely a clue to identify the site. When the question provides a named person as an example using constructions like 'like [Named Person]' or 'such as [Named Person],' the named person exemplifies a role or title — answer with that role or title (e.g., 'the editor,' 'the president') rather than the specific named person.
why: Student answered 'English Muffin' when gold was 'pizza' for 'In cooking class, you'll learn that an English muffin & tomato sauce are the starting points of a miniature this.' The student saw 'pizza' in the context ('miniature this | pizza') and misapplied Rule 006, treating 'pizza' as redundant context to be stripped. But 'this' is the question target — it refers to the entity being described (pizza), not a modifier. Rule 002's existing guidance to 'identify what grammatical role that pronoun plays' was insufficient; the student failed to recognize that 'this' at the end of a question is the answer entity itself, not subject to redundancy-stripping. Added sub-condition to clarify this case.

[RULE 003] Prefer primary entity over exhaustive lists
content: When a question describes multiple instances, roles, or items but there is a single primary entity underlying them (either because the context emphasizes one as primary, or because the question refers to a single subject with multiple attributes), answer with that primary entity rather than listing all mentioned instances or items.
why: Original case: question used plural phrasing 'these animals' and context emphasized 'particularly sheep,' but student listed all animals instead of just 'Sheep.' Current case: question described multiple roles across years ('In '84 he's a destroyer, in '82 he was just a barbarian') referring to a single character, but student listed both movie titles instead of just 'Conan.' The rule's trigger was too narrow—it only fired on plural phrasing, missing cases where singular phrasing describes multiple instances of one entity.

[RULE 004] Preserve middle initials in person names
content: When a person's full name appears in the context with a middle initial, include the middle initial in the answer rather than dropping it for conciseness — but only if the middle initial is part of how the person is commonly known (e.g., 'Dwight D. Eisenhower,' 'John F. Kennedy'). If the person is predominantly known without the middle initial (e.g., 'Alan Shepard,' 'Neil Armstrong'), use the shorter form without the initial.
why: Original case: student answered 'Dwight Eisenhower' when gold was 'Dwight D. Eisenhower' — the middle initial 'D.' is integral to how Eisenhower is commonly known, so it should be preserved. Current case: both student and teacher answered 'Alan B. Shepard, Jr.' when gold was 'Alan Shepard' — the middle initial 'B.' and suffix 'Jr.' are not part of how Shepard is commonly known, so the shorter form should be used. The rule was too absolute; it needs to distinguish between cases where the middle initial is integral to the person's commonly known name vs. cases where it is not.

[RULE 005] Parse Jeopardy category-clue format correctly
content: When context presents a Jeopardy-style entry in the format 'CATEGORY - Clue text', recognize that the text before the hyphen is the category name (not the answer), and determine the answer by identifying what entity the clue text describes.
why: Student answered 'Six Flags' when the gold was 'Cyprus' because it misinterpreted 'SIX FLAGS' in 'Jeopardy Question: SIX FLAGS - Troubled Mediterranean island nation whose flag is seen here' as the answer, when actually 'SIX FLAGS' is the Jeopardy category and the clue describes Cyprus. The teacher correctly treated the pre-hyphen text as a category label and answered based on the clue content.

[RULE 006] Avoid redundant context in answers
content: When a question already mentions identifying context (brand name, location, category, or any qualifying term) or when an entity is commonly known by a shorter name, answer with that shorter, commonly known form rather than restating context, adding type descriptors, repeating qualifiers already present in the question, or using the full formal name. For titles of works (TV shows, films, books) with prepositional phrases or subtitles, drop the qualifier and use the shorter core title (e.g., 'on the Sunset Strip' from 'Studio 60 on the Sunset Strip' → 'Studio 60') even if the context only provides the full formal title; this shorter core title is the expected answer and is not considered ambiguous. For type descriptors, strip product-type words from company/brand names (e.g., 'Cards' from 'Hallmark Cards' → 'Hallmark') just as you would strip geographic type descriptors (e.g., 'River' from 'Amazon River' → 'Amazon', or 'Mountains' from 'Adirondack Mountains' → 'Adirondacks' when the question says 'these mountains'), event type descriptors when the question already names the event type (e.g., 'Conference' from 'Yalta Conference' when the question says 'the 1945 conference' → 'Yalta'), market type descriptors when the question already names the market type (e.g., 'Market' from 'Bear Market' when the question says 'this market' → 'bear'), nutrient type descriptors when the question already names the nutrient type (e.g., 'Vitamin' from 'Vitamin C' when the question says 'this vitamin' → 'C'), or organization type descriptors when the question already names the organization type (e.g., 'Party' from 'Bull Moose Party' → 'Bull Moose' when the question says 'Progressive party'). This applies whether the type descriptor appears before or after the identifier. However, do NOT strip the type descriptor if the shortened form would be ambiguous or refer to a different entity category (e.g., keep 'Ace Hardware' rather than shortening to 'Ace,' since 'Ace' alone is ambiguous; keep 'Grasshopper pie' rather than shortening to 'Grasshopper,' since 'Grasshopper' alone refers to an insect, not a pie), or when the type descriptor is an integral part of the entity's standard common name (e.g., keep 'eucalyptus tree' rather than shortening to 'Eucalyptus,' since 'eucalyptus tree' is the standard common name), even if the question's context makes the intended meaning clear. Additionally, do not strip a state or country name that follows a city name (e.g., keep 'Anchorage, Alaska' rather than shortening to 'Anchorage') as this is the standard expected answer format, unless the question already explicitly names that state or country. For location-derived modifiers (demonyms), strip the modifier when the location is already mentioned in the question (e.g., 'Maltese' from 'Maltese Falcon' when the question mentions 'Malta' → 'falcon'). For person names in trivia questions, use the name as provided in the context without adding or removing parts. If the context provides only a surname, use that surname; do not add a first name from external knowledge. If multiple name variants appear, prefer the formal name (e.g., 'Patricia' not 'Patty') over nicknames, and include honorific titles (e.g., 'Sir,' 'Lord') when they are part of the person's commonly known formal name (e.g., 'Sir Isaac Newton' rather than just 'Isaac Newton'). Use surname alone when the person is predominantly known by surname (e.g., 'Brutus' or 'Plato') or when no other person with the same surname appears in context. When the question already mentions part of a person's name (e.g., their first name), answer with only the remaining part (e.g., the surname) rather than repeating the full name.
why: Both student and teacher answered 'Anchorage' when gold was 'Anchorage, Alaska' for a question asking for 'the largest city in its state'. The agents misapplied Rule 006 by treating the state name 'Alaska' as redundant context to be stripped, even though the question only said 'in its state' without explicitly naming Alaska. Added an exception to clarify that state or country names following a city name should not be stripped unless the question explicitly names that state or country, as 'City, State' is the standard expected answer format.

[RULE 007] Match answer number to dominant context form
content: When a question asks for an entity and the context predominantly uses the plural form of that entity (e.g., 'strawberries' appears multiple times), answer with the plural form even if the question phrasing uses singular (e.g., 'this fruit').
why: Both student and teacher answered 'strawberry' (singular) when the gold was 'strawberries' (plural). The question said 'this fruit' (singular), leading agents to use singular, but the context heavily uses the plural form ('million strawberries,' etc.). The agent should match the dominant grammatical number in context rather than inferring number from question phrasing.

[RULE 008] Answer entity-list questions with shared location or organization
content: When a question consists only of a list of entity names without an explicit question word or verb, the question is asking what the entities have in common. When the question begins with 'In' followed by a list (e.g., 'In Dijon & Bordeaux'), the expected answer is the adjective or demonym form describing what quality or nationality the entities share (e.g., 'French' rather than 'France') — answer with the adjective form, not the location name. For entity-list questions without 'In,' identify shared attributes and answer with the shared location (state, region, country) or organization, rather than a shared role or profession. When a question is just a single place name without an explicit question word or verb (e.g., 'Hamburg'), answer with the country or containing location (e.g., 'Germany'), not the type of place (e.g., 'city'). When a question is just a title followed by a person's name without an explicit question word or verb (e.g., 'President Tran Duc Luong'), answer with the country or organization associated with that title (e.g., 'Vietnam'), not the person's name or the full title phrase.
why: Student answered 'Tran Duc Luong' when gold was 'Vietnam' for the bare phrase question 'President Tran Duc Luong.' The student repeated the person's name from the question, and the teacher answered 'President of Vietnam' (including the redundant title). This is the same bare-phrase pattern Rule 008 already handles for single place names (Hamburg → Germany) and entity lists — a question without a question word or verb expects the associated location/organization, not a repetition of the named entity or its full title. Extended the rule to cover titled-person phrases.

[RULE 009] Answer location codes with location names
content: When a question provides a location code or abbreviation (such as an airport code like PHL), answer with the full name of the location that the code represents, not an entity or landmark at that location.
why: Both student and teacher answered 'Liberty Bell' when the gold was 'Philadelphia' for 'Let freedom ring: PHL.' The question provides the airport code PHL and the answer is the location name it represents; the agents incorrectly answered with an entity at that location rather than the location name itself.

[RULE 010] Recognize quote and partial-phrase questions
content: When a question is a partial phrase marked by ellipsis (e.g., '...on my mind') or a quote that is cut off mid-sentence (e.g., ending with a conjunction like 'and' or a preposition), recognize it as a fill-in-the-blank reference and answer with the missing word or element that completes the quote — do not answer with the title of the work or other metadata. When a cut-off quote is followed by 'this,' the word 'this' is the placeholder for the missing word, not a pointer to the work's title. When a question is a full quote or a standalone line from a work, answer with the author or creator of the work, not the title of the work itself. When a question contains a phrase that the context explains is a spoonerism, speech error, or wordplay (even when phrased as a declarative statement), answer with the intended original phrase the speaker meant to say — use only the core intended words (e.g., 'dear old queen'), not the garbled version, not the person who said it, and not the name of the wordplay concept.
why: Student answered 'Reverend William Archibald Spooner' when gold was 'Dear old queen' for 'Dr. Spooner once talked of his queer old dean.' The context explains 'queer old dean' is a spoonerism for 'dear old queen.' The teacher correctly recognized the spoonerism pattern and answered with the intended phrase. Rule 010 covered quotes and partial phrases but not spoonerisms/speech errors where the question presents a garbled phrase and the answer is the intended original phrase.

[RULE 011] Match definition questions to precise term or number
content: When a question provides a specific defining characteristic, description, or numerical claim (e.g., 'A house with floors that differ by about half a story' or 'accounts for a third of exports'), identify which candidate in the context precisely matches that claim. If the most prominent candidate's statistics don't match the specific number (e.g., 22% or 47% instead of 'a third'), consider alternative candidates that might match, even if they are described as less prominent or 'formerly' important.
why: Student answered 'Coffee' when gold was 'Bananas' for 'This fruit crop accounts for a third of Honduras' exports & is the country's leading source of income.' The context said coffee was 22% or 47% of exports, which doesn't match 'a third' (33%). Both agents focused on the word 'leading' and matched it to 'Leading export coffee,' without checking if the numerical claim matched. The rule's original trigger only covered descriptive definitions; it needed to explicitly include numerical claims and instruct agents to consider alternatives when the most prominent candidate's statistics don't match the specific number.

[RULE 012] Prefer original title over translation
content: When context presents a work (film, book, etc.) with both an original foreign-language title and an English translation or release title, answer with the original title rather than the English translation.
why: Student answered 'The Postman' when gold was 'Il Postino.' The context explicitly labeled 'Il Postino' as the original title and 'The Postman' as the English release title, but the student chose the English translation. The teacher correctly used the original title.

[RULE 013] Resolve possessive pronouns in statement questions
content: When a question is a declarative statement containing a possessive pronoun ('his,' 'her,' 'their'), the question is asking for the person the pronoun refers to — answer with that person, not the work, event, or other entity named in the statement.
why: Original case: student answered 'En folkefiende' when gold was 'Henrik Ibsen' for a statement-question where 'his' referred to the author. Current case: student answered 'Treaty of Paris' when gold was 'Napoleon' for 'The 1815 Treaty of Paris was signed after his final defeat at Waterloo.' The student treated the question as asking for the treaty name (applying Rule 005/006), when the question was actually asking who 'his' refers to — the person whose defeat at Waterloo led to the treaty. Rule 013's original trigger was too narrow — it only covered possessive pronouns referring to creators/authors of works, missing cases where the pronoun refers to a historical figure associated with an event.

[RULE 014] Answer tribute band questions with original artist

why: Student answered 'U2 Tribute Band' and 'Achtung Babies' when gold was 'U2' for 'Rock group Achtung Babies' because the agent treated the tribute band's name or functional description as the answer, whereas the question expects the original artist being tributed.

[RULE 015] Answer adaptation questions with source material
content: When a question names or describes a creative work (film, musical, book, etc.) and the context states that the work is an adaptation, re-telling, or version of another work, answer with the source material or original work it is based on, not the named work itself or metadata about it (such as the director or year).
why: Student answered 'West Side Story' and teacher answered 'Robert Wise' when gold was 'Romeo and Juliet' for 'A 1961 classic: West Side Story.' The context explicitly describes West Side Story as 'a modern-day, loose re-telling of Shakespeare's Romeo and Juliet tragedy' and 'the award-winning adaptation of the classic romantic tragedy, Romeo and Juliet.' Both agents answered with the named work or its metadata instead of recognizing that the question asks for the source material the named work is based on.
